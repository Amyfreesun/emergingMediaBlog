Blog Entry 1: September 8, 2026

“In Defense of the Poor Image” contemplates how a hierarchy of images based on resolution can be read as a classist issue. There is an assumption that some images are inherently better or more valuable than others because they are “higher quality”, sharper, or closer to the original. But why do we judge some images as lesser?

There is something beautiful about the so-called degradation of an image. How many hands has it passed through? Why was it shared so many times? What does it mean for an image to become softer and less sharp as it moves further away from the original? That degradation can actually be evidence of circulation, use, and access.

There are also practical reasons for keeping images and media files smaller. They are easier to share, download, and distribute. The demand for perfect, high-resolution images can become a form of control and gatekeeping. Controlling access to the “original” or highest-quality version of photographs and cinema creates a hierarchy and preserves the ability to profit from it. This becomes especially significant when the media has revolutionary or political potential. Restricting access can be a way of locking up alternate messages and hiding the key.

With the enormous number of poor images circulating, everyone can have a hand in their distribution. The distinction between producer and audience becomes less clear as people download, repost, edit, compress, and redistribute images. At the same time, these images exist within capitalism. They are part of a large pool of media constantly being used to sell things and circulate alongside a culture of excess and overconsumption. The poor image can resist hierarchy through accessibility and collective distribution, while also becoming another part of the endless stream of content and consumption. These poor images are without ownership or stewardship, a state from which chaos can emerge and images (and the mugs they belong to) are destined to lose control of their original narrative.

In the reading “In Defense of the Poor Image”, the author seems to suggest that the state of our images reflects our own reality. The accessibility of poor images creates simultaneous opportunities for progressive possibilities… as well as “porn and paranoia.” The image itself is simply out there, available to be circulated and used toward pluralistic definitions of both good and evil.

Blog Entry 2: September 14, 2026

As I watched the video for the Telegarden, the next video in the queue was titled “Ex-Anthropic Insider Tells CNN How AI Could Kill All Humans by 2030,” under the header “Apocalyptic Warning.” The pairing of this video with the Walter Benjamin essay feels particularly fitting. From Benjamin’s age of mechanical reproduction, we have entered into an era of AI replacement. Where cameras helped us see in new ways, AI has the potential to alter not only how we think, but potentially our very capacity to think for ourselves. Benjamin wrote about what happens when technology changes human perception. This next phase of technology raises the question of what happens when technology becomes an active participant in the process of perception and interpretation itself. When a tool designed to expand human capacity begins to replace it, is it actually still helping to advance humanity? And if there is no human involved in the making of art, can the art still have an aura?

I feel nervous and embarrassed admitting that I use AI. Mostly, I see it as a conversational partner instead of a thought dictator. In lieu of a teacher, colleague, or even a friend to discuss ideas with, I find it helpful to bounce ideas off ChatGPT and then refine my own thinking and definitely my wording. It still feels shameful because I know that AI data centers have environmental and social consequences, and I worry about what using AI might mean for my own mental and artistic development. Despite having strong relationships in my life, they are hard to access with any sort of immediacy. Previous technologies taught me to expect that immediacy. Once upon a time, I had easy access to long conversations through AIM, Messenger, and gChat. Those have faded from my reality, and though I still text, I no longer have those same long conversations over the computer with humans. In contrast, AI is always online and ready to respond and collaborate. While I still feel ownership over the ideas I develop through these conversations, I also acknowledge the paradox that the same technology I turn to when I feel isolated may push me deeper into my isolation from others and potentially from my own thought processes. As my AI thought partner says, “Technological reproduction is never simply liberating or destructive. The possibilities and the losses arrive together.”

Blog Entry: September 21, 2026

This article really puts the rapid pace of technological advancement into perspective. So many of Bush’s ideas were spot on, and many of the technologies that emerged have far exceeded what probably seemed wildly futuristic, if not impossible, at the time. What is especially strange is how ordinary some of these technologies now feel. Things like GoPros, voice-to-text, and hashtags are so integrated into everyday life that they no longer seem revolutionary or even particularly mind-boggling.

It makes me wonder what the equivalent technologies of the future will be. Hopefully they don’t look too much like an episode of Black Mirror. Is uploading our consciousness possible? Colonizing Mars? What sounds completely absurd now that might feel mundane fifty years from now? And what are the ideas that are so absurd that no one has even imagined them yet? Eventually someone has to imagine something before it can be brought into existence. Except, maybe, that relationship will change with AI, as future technologies might be developed increasingly apart from human imagination. That makes the future seem even more precarious and impossible to predict.

But I also wonder if predicting the technology itself is actually easier than predicting what it will do to us. Bush imagined many of the tools we now use, but could he have imagined the enormous power social media would have to influence elections or shape our sense of reality?

When I was a kid, the technology that sounded coolest to me was a personal, portable TV, which of course is now fairly standard for many children in the form of an iPad. But the surprising part isn’t really that we figured out how to make a television portable. It is everything else that device has become and the role it now plays in childhood. Technology can be a little like candy: incredibly tempting and immediately gratifying, but capable of producing a serious sugar crash. Every new technology seems to come with some kind of trade-off, which also connects to our discussion of Walter Benjamin.

And also: Bush could imagine so many extraordinary things, but he still imagined the scientist as a man and a “roomful of girls armed with simple keyboard punches.” His technological imagination could reach far into the future, while his social imagination was still very much rooted in his own time. Maybe that is another limitation in how we imagine the future. We tend to picture incredible new machines while assuming that the people using them, and the social structures surrounding them, will remain more or less the same. My wildest hopes are actually for what sometimes seems most impossible in the social sphere: that humanity, as a whole, might move toward greater empathy and equality - which seems far more revolutionary and impactful than any one technology.

Blog Entry 4: September 29, 2026

Debord feels incredibly prescient. It is hard to believe he was writing this in the 1960s, before social media. Could he even have imagined the extremes our society could reach? And how much further can we go? According to Debord, the spectacle pervasively invades our lived reality, and though its impact isn’t uniform, it becomes part of our social fabric. This sort of theory feels a bit like The Matrix. Is escape possible?

This essay relates closely to the clothing project in that it examines how image and consumption are intrinsically linked. The desire to buy is far from a need to survive or even thrive. It is a manufactured need based on a manufactured desire to achieve a representation of reality that can never actualize because it doesn’t actually exist. The clothing represents the idea of becoming a person, a feeling, a version of oneself. But the obsessive need for more relates to the “sun that never sets over the empire of modern passivity,” and to a belief that the commodity can somehow save us from the dungeon our economy has built for its little consumers. There can never be a final purchase that gets you there because what would feed the hungry economy then? The cycle of consumption has no end because the machine must be maintained.

As I consider my own consumption, it is helpful to realize that the monster isn’t just what I see in the mirror. It is this spectacle of “having,” this “main production” of society, this modern industry in which “the goal is nothing, development is all.” The spectacle becomes a cruel dom who subjugates her little consumers in an ever-isolating, hypnotic spiral, feeding on their desire for connection while further alienating them in their endless chase to become the impossible image.

There are different roles within the spectacle, but unless you are completely off-grid, it seems impossible not to partake in the ensemble and be implicated. So what does an awareness of these constructs actually achieve? Does it give us the ability to create tools to deconstruct them and kill the internalized images that have become real beings in our minds? To detach the self from the brunt of it? To reduce harm? To increase happiness by LIVING rather than being consumed by a pseudo-reality mediated through images? Is awareness itself a way of waking up from The Matrix? And what could follow the fall of the all-powerful economy of late-stage capitalism?

Blog Entry 5: October 6, 2026

After reading this article and Enzensberger’s scathing critiques of pretty much everyone, I was curious about how he lived his life. Was he a political revolutionary who found a way to spread his message via a thorough understanding of technology/popular media? (I wondered how successfully he had done this, considering I had never heard of him before.) Is this essay a call to arms that he is hoping other future folks can someday actualize? Would those folks be from the “masses” or other literati?

TBH, I find the density of Marxist theory really difficult, both literally and otherwise, especially having worked as an Interpretation Specialist, where my job was to translate curatorial academic texts into accessible didactics. In all my higher education experience, no one had really explained to me the point of theory. Recently, I had a helpful conversation with a buddy in traditional academia who explained that this kind of writing isn’t necessarily willfully obtuse, but is often a way of using shorthand to build on more than 100 years of theory. The theorists are in the business of producing knowledge - often within an insular academic community, hopefully to be consumed and translated into action by policy makers and more digestible materials by educators. Some academics even go so far as to shield themselves from real world applications in order to maintain the purity of their scientific research.

However, in Enzensberger’s case, it seemed especially ironic that he used this kind of prose while critiquing the Left for not knowing how to connect with the masses through popular media and for relying on antiquated methods of communication. While he acknowledges the alienating features of writing, ultimately this dense theoretical text is the medium through which his ideas reached me. He really seems to abhor McLuhan despite seeing some merit in “the medium is the message.” But I wonder, what is the message Enzensberger is elevating by using dense theory as his medium? How could this text itself be translated into something more accessible and participatory?

Obviously, the text is very prescient. Enzensberger knows some form of media is coming that is more participatory, and he wants the Left to be prepared to engage with it. But he also knows it isn’t just about the media. It is about what you do with it. Even when everyone becomes a producer, if production isn’t actually a collective process that challenges capitalism and political power, it isn’t living up to its potential for mobilization. It is the combination of having something political to say, having a way to share that message that reaches the masses, and creating a way for the masses to engage in the conversation.

After reading about Enzensberger’s life, I learned that he grew up in Nazi Germany, was briefly in the Hitler Youth until he was kicked out, and later deserted after being drafted near the end of the war. So, distrust in authority makes sense. He traveled to Cuba and became disillusioned when he found that it wasn’t the kind of participatory society he had hoped to see come to fruition. He was also a poet, worked in radio, and founded a leftist journal. When he critiques the Left, he is critiquing a movement he is part of and essentially telling his comrades, “Hey, we should be using media differently.”

I can see how this text itself isn’t meant to be the kind of mass media Enzensberger is advocating for, but is instead directed at the people he thinks could create it. In that case, using the language of theory makes more sense, even if there is still something ironic about relying on the kind of specialized intellectual culture he spends so much time critiquing.

I would also be interested in seeing what kind of radio he produced and if there is any media being made today that meets his standards. The communications tools to make everyone a producer exist but it doesn’t seem as if the truly collective process he envisioned has been actualized. What is holding us back?

Blog Entry: October 19, 2026

Donna Haraway, A Cyborg Manifesto

This reading makes me think of Suzie Silver and her Extra Terrestrial Sexuality project, and now I am wondering if she was inspired by this essay. I think that it is liberating to have a non-humanoid framework to turn our assumptions on their heads by making the familiar appear alien. I participated in that compilation, and it was helpful for me to analyze my own intimate desires through abstraction and by looking at insects and relationships between supposedly inanimate objects for inspiration. There was something exciting about temporarily abandoning the rules and categories that we use to make sense of the world, especially when it comes to sexuality and intimacy. It makes me wonder how much of what we understand as natural is actually determined by the limits of our imagination.

How can you imagine a color you've never seen? I am so so so so curious when people say they have dreamed of a new color. Is it actually possible to imagine something completely outside our frame of reference, or are we always rearranging familiar elements into new combinations? I think this is part of what makes the cyborg such an interesting metaphor. It gives us permission to imagine ourselves outside the boundaries of the known, even if we can't quite envision what exists beyond them.

I have also been very inspired by Octavia Butler's quote, "There is nothing new under the sun, but there are new suns." And one of her heroines, Lauren Olamina, looks to the stars as our destiny. Of course, fixating outside our atmosphere can be dangerous when it means completely abandoning any hope of salvaging a dying Earth, like the bet on Mars that many of our tech-bro billionaires seem to be hedging. But I also appreciate the possibility of looking beyond our current reality as a way of imagining different futures, rather than simply escaping the problems we've created here.

Butler's Xenogenesis series is also very relevant to A Cyborg Manifesto. In it, humanity's only hope for survival is to crossbreed with the alien Oankali and accept that humanity as it was once known is dead. The Oankali live in enormous living ships, manipulate DNA almost like painting, and view humanity's fatal flaw as the combination of intelligence and a predisposition toward hierarchy. They are not interested in preserving humanity in its original form and the only way for humans to procreate is as a chimera. The resulting relationships between humans and aliens are sometimes beautiful but are complicated by questions of consent and power.

This feels very relevant to Haraway's cyborg in that humanity has to change to survive. We can't go on as we have. The way that we are living is changing the Earth, which in turn changes us and how we interact with the tricky and maybe imaginary boundary between nature and machine/technology. "God is Change," as Butler writes. "All that you touch You Change. All that you Change Changes you." Butler again.

The question for me is: How much influence do we actually have over how we change, and how can we use our chimeric powers to shape our own narratives?

October 26, 2026

Reading Response: Critical Art Ensemble, The Electronic Disturbance

This essay was written in 1994, before the mall became the screen, and CAE was already describing the economy of desire as being “safely viewed through the familiar window of screenal space” and discussing malls in much the same language. Malls and the couch were the sanctuaries of my youth. Where else did you hang out as a tween in the 90s? Consumption and TV are salves for an anxiety-ridden, dangerous world. And the salves before? Religion? Religion has taken different paths and had vastly different impacts, of course. Spirituality and connection on one hand, mass genocide and hysteria on the other. CAE makes connections between virtual space and the invisible worlds of religion and imagination.

As chimeric cyborgs, how can we create comfort without malls and screens, without simply inverting the systems that keep us ensnared and complacent within the liquid power structures of occupation and consumption?

Neural pathways get cemented. Do we need a jackhammer? How do we create new ways of connecting and finding comfort that break away from the screen? I am disturbed as I watch how easily screens calm my children, recalling nights of quotidian, post-breakup (or deeper existential terror-induced) hysterical sobbing muted by a good stream. My children teach me every day how presence is the greatest gift, often through my own difficulty in abiding their desire for my attention.

I realize that my reading responses are very self-reflective and individualistic, as is my own art project. I've been focused on consumption throughout these readings, though I also acknowledge the concurrent/interconnected themes, like occupation and the way nomadic power now operates through corporations. Simultaneously, I see how it might behoove me to be more community-focused in my approach (to the readings, to my art, to my life...). But I also wonder whether recognizing how I have been seduced by these systems is a way to begin disentangling myself from them. Maybe they need to happen concurrently.

I wish it were easier to divorce myself from the screen, but my life is so enmeshed with it that I don't know how to function without the ability to Google. Remembering the mall, I don't think I had some utopic youth either. While I was playing in the creek more, these addictions, domineering systems, and complacencies already existed. From gothic arches to golden arches to some social media metaphor of arches.

Maybe one step is simply cultivating a presence of mind outside of these systems, an ability to be with others as well as within oneself. Some sort of nomadic mindscape, an ability to unplug from the desire machines.
