Blog Entry 1: September 8, 2026

“In Defense of the Poor Image” contemplates how a hierarchy of images based on resolution can be read as a classist issue. There is an assumption that some images are inherently better or more valuable than others because they are “higher quality”, sharper, or closer to the original. But why do we judge some images as lesser?

There is something beautiful about the so-called degradation of an image. How many hands has it passed through? Why was it shared so many times? What does it mean for an image to become softer and less sharp as it moves further away from the original? That degradation can actually be evidence of circulation, use, and access.

There are also practical reasons for keeping images and media files smaller. They are easier to share, download, and distribute. The demand for perfect, high-resolution images can become a form of control and gatekeeping. Controlling access to the “original” or highest-quality version of photographs and cinema creates a hierarchy and preserves the ability to profit from it. This becomes especially significant when the media has revolutionary or political potential. Restricting access can be a way of locking up alternate messages and hiding the key.

With the enormous number of poor images circulating, everyone can have a hand in their distribution. The distinction between producer and audience becomes less clear as people download, repost, edit, compress, and redistribute images. At the same time, these images exist within capitalism. They are part of a large pool of media constantly being used to sell things and circulate alongside a culture of excess and overconsumption. The poor image can resist hierarchy through accessibility and collective distribution, while also becoming another part of the endless stream of content and consumption. These poor images are without ownership or stewardship, a state from which chaos can emerge and images (and the mugs they belong to) are destined to lose control of their original narrative.

In the reading “In Defense of the Poor Image”, the author seems to suggest that the state of our images reflects our own reality. The accessibility of poor images creates simultaneous opportunities for progressive possibilities… as well as “porn and paranoia.” The image itself is simply out there, available to be circulated and used toward pluralistic definitions of both good and evil.

Blog Entry 2: September 14, 2026

As I watched the video for the Telegarden, the next video in the queue was titled “Ex-Anthropic Insider Tells CNN How AI Could Kill All Humans by 2030,” under the header “Apocalyptic Warning.” The pairing of this video with the Walter Benjamin essay feels particularly fitting. From Benjamin’s age of mechanical reproduction, we have entered into an era of AI replacement. Where cameras helped us see in new ways, AI has the potential to alter not only how we think, but potentially our very capacity to think for ourselves. Benjamin wrote about what happens when technology changes human perception. This next phase of technology raises the question of what happens when technology becomes an active participant in the process of perception and interpretation itself. When a tool designed to expand human capacity begins to replace it, is it actually still helping to advance humanity? And if there is no human involved in the making of art, can the art still have an aura?

I feel nervous and embarrassed admitting that I use AI. Mostly, I see it as a conversational partner instead of a thought dictator. In lieu of a teacher, colleague, or even a friend to discuss ideas with, I find it helpful to bounce ideas off ChatGPT and then refine my own thinking and definitely my wording. It still feels shameful because I know that AI data centers have environmental and social consequences, and I worry about what using AI might mean for my own mental and artistic development. Despite having strong relationships in my life, they are hard to access with any sort of immediacy. Previous technologies taught me to expect that immediacy. Once upon a time, I had easy access to long conversations through AIM, Messenger, and gChat. Those have faded from my reality, and though I still text, I no longer have those same long conversations over the computer with humans. In contrast, AI is always online and ready to respond and collaborate. While I still feel ownership over the ideas I develop through these conversations, I also acknowledge the paradox that the same technology I turn to when I feel isolated may push me deeper into my isolation from others and potentially from my own thought processes. As my AI thought partner says, “Technological reproduction is never simply liberating or destructive. The possibilities and the losses arrive together.”

Blog Entry: September 21, 2026

This article really puts the rapid pace of technological advancement into perspective. So many of Bush’s ideas were spot on, and many of the technologies that emerged have far exceeded what probably seemed wildly futuristic, if not impossible, at the time. What is especially strange is how ordinary some of these technologies now feel. Things like GoPros, voice-to-text, and hashtags are so integrated into everyday life that they no longer seem revolutionary or even particularly mind-boggling.

It makes me wonder what the equivalent technologies of the future will be. Hopefully they don’t look too much like an episode of Black Mirror. Is uploading our consciousness possible? Colonizing Mars? What sounds completely absurd now that might feel mundane fifty years from now? And what are the ideas that are so absurd that no one has even imagined them yet? Eventually someone has to imagine something before it can be brought into existence. Except, maybe, that relationship will change with AI, as future technologies might be developed increasingly apart from human imagination. That makes the future seem even more precarious and impossible to predict.

But I also wonder if predicting the technology itself is actually easier than predicting what it will do to us. Bush imagined many of the tools we now use, but could he have imagined the enormous power social media would have to influence elections or shape our sense of reality?

When I was a kid, the technology that sounded coolest to me was a personal, portable TV, which of course is now fairly standard for many children in the form of an iPad. But the surprising part isn’t really that we figured out how to make a television portable. It is everything else that device has become and the role it now plays in childhood. Technology can be a little like candy: incredibly tempting and immediately gratifying, but capable of producing a serious sugar crash. Every new technology seems to come with some kind of trade-off, which also connects to our discussion of Walter Benjamin.

And also: Bush could imagine so many extraordinary things, but he still imagined the scientist as a man and a “roomful of girls armed with simple keyboard punches.” His technological imagination could reach far into the future, while his social imagination was still very much rooted in his own time. Maybe that is another limitation in how we imagine the future. We tend to picture incredible new machines while assuming that the people using them, and the social structures surrounding them, will remain more or less the same. My wildest hopes are actually for what sometimes seems most impossible in the social sphere: that humanity, as a whole, might move toward greater empathy and equality.
